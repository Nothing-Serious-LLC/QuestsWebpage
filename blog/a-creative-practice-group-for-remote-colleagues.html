A Creative Practice Group for Remote Colleagues

Planning and templates Nov 23, 2026 · By Quests

Run a remote creative-practice group with one optional prompt, a personal time limit and an asynchronous way to respond. Keep the activity separate from work assessment and give people a clear choice about sharing. This two-week example works with an existing chat or document and ordinary materials.

Use the six prompts as a starting point. Someone can sketch, write or keep private observations while following the same theme as colleagues. The schedule and agreement below help you decide how the group will work.

Agree on four decisions

  1. Time: each participant chooses a session length that fits. In the example, that is between five and ten minutes.
  2. Schedule: Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday are suggested practice days. People may pick different days, including weekdays only.
  3. Sharing: anyone can share a short reflection, an image they own or simply that they tried the exercise. Keeping the work private is also an option.
  4. Feedback: ask before offering critique. Encouragement and a question can be enough.

Decide explicitly whether any practice happens during paid work time. Check that with the appropriate workplace contact. People can choose an off-work arrangement without an expectation that colleagues will give up personal time.

Six prompts with different ways to take part

  1. Notice one ordinary object. Sketch its outline, describe it in three sentences or record an observation in your own notes. Use an object you are comfortable sharing.
  2. Make a small change. Redraw an object with one exaggerated feature, or rewrite a sentence from a different point of view. Keep the earlier version for your own comparison.
  3. Collect three details. Choose three colors, textures or sounds in your immediate surroundings. Turn them into a tiny written scene or a simple pattern.
  4. Work within one constraint. Try a drawing using circles, a six-line story or a design using two colors. Choose a constraint that suits your tools and access needs.
  5. Return to an earlier attempt. Add one detail, remove one sentence or try a different arrangement. Record what you chose to change.
  6. Choose a next experiment. Pick a prompt to repeat or invent your own. Write one sentence about what you would like to explore next.

These are invitations to practice. There is no required standard of artistic quality, and participants can substitute an accessible activity. Use paper, a notes app or materials already available. Keep client work, confidential documents and other people's faces or identifying details out of shared examples.

A worked schedule for three colleagues

Nia works in London and chooses Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday mornings. Omar works in Toronto and chooses Monday, Wednesday and Friday evenings. Bea works a changing schedule and books two available sessions each week. They use the six prompts in order, with Bea choosing four favorites.

Over two weeks, Nia plans six sessions, Omar six and Bea four. The group has 16 personal practice opportunities. Each person records their own progress. On the two Fridays, anyone who wants to can post a sentence about an observation. They agree that a Friday post can arrive during that person's local day.

Nia completes five sessions, Omar four and Bea four. Those invented records total 13 of 16 opportunities, about 81%. This is a description of the example, with no ranking between colleagues. A voluntary group can also skip totals entirely and keep only reflections. Our time-zone worksheet helps make a more complicated schedule explicit.

Copy an invitation and a feedback reply

Would you like to try a two-week creative-practice group? There are six optional prompts, and you can choose your own days and session length. Sharing work is optional. We will ask before offering feedback, and participation will stay separate from work evaluation. You can change your plan or stop whenever you need.

For a response, try: “Thanks for sharing this. Would you like encouragement, a question or a suggestion?” If the person wants a question, ask something specific, such as “What made you choose those two colors?” Keep your interpretation tentative and let them decide how much to discuss.

Handle a missed session or a changing schedule

If someone misses a session, they can return at their next planned time. If their availability changes, update the remaining schedule and record when it changed. Ask before sending a reminder. A colleague who stops participating can leave without supplying a personal explanation.

Use the supportive-message examples if the group wants reminders, and the different-goals guide when people want different activities.

Download the setup sheet

Download the remote creative-practice worksheet. It contains the six prompts, an individual schedule and a group agreement. Fill it in together or keep separate copies.

At the end, ask whether the activity was worth the time, whether the sharing arrangement felt comfortable and what each person wants next. Record only what people volunteer.
