How to Start a Challenge With Friends That Everyone Finishes

Guide Sep 18, 2026 · 8 min read

Most group challenges die the same way. Six people say yes in the group chat, four start, two are still checking in by day 12, and by day 20 nobody mentions it. The idea was fine. The setup was wrong.

This guide is the setup. It covers what to choose, how to write the rules, how big the group should be, where the check-ins should live, and what to do when life happens to one of you.

Step 1: Choose one habit with a daily yes or no

The challenge needs a single action that each person can confirm at the end of the day. "Get healthier" cannot be checked in on. "Walk 8,000 steps" can. "Read more" cannot. "Read 20 pages" can.

Binary rules matter more in a group than alone, because the group needs to agree on what counts. Every fuzzy rule becomes a negotiation on day nine.

If the group cannot agree on one habit, run a shared rule with personal targets: everyone does "30 minutes of movement," and each person decides what movement means for them. Our list of 50 healthy challenge ideas is written in this format.

Step 2: Set a length and an end date

Open-ended habits have no finish line, so there is nothing to celebrate and no natural moment to recommit. Pick a length:

  • 7 days to test whether the group likes the habit.
  • 14 days for a reset (sleep, sugar, screens).
  • 30 days for a habit you want to keep. This is the most common length and the one we designed Quests around.
  • 66 to 90 days if you already know you want it permanent. The research on habit formation puts the median at 66 days.

Start on a Monday or the first of the month. Arbitrary, and it works: shared start dates feel like events.

Step 3: Keep the group between two and five

Two is the minimum for accountability. One partner is enough to double your odds, and it is fragile: if they drop, you are alone. Three to five is the sweet spot. Everyone's check-in is still visible and missed, and one dropout does not end the challenge.

Above five, people start to hide. Nobody notices one missing check-in in a group of twelve, and the visibility that makes group challenges work disappears. If you have a big group, split it into pods of four.

Step 4: Write the rules down before day one

Three sentences, agreed in writing:

  1. The daily action and what counts. ("20 pages of a book. Audiobooks count at 20 minutes. Articles do not.")
  2. The deadline for a check-in. ("Before midnight, your local time.")
  3. What a miss means. ("A miss resets your streak. It does not remove you from the challenge. Two misses in a row and the group checks on you.")

The third rule is the one people skip and the one that saves the most challenges. Decide now that a missed day is a dent, so nobody quits out of embarrassment on day 11.

Step 5: Give the check-ins their own home

A group chat feels like the obvious place. It works for about a week. Then the check-ins get buried under weekend plans and nobody can tell who has done today's action without scrolling.

The check-in needs a dedicated place where the whole group's status is visible at a glance. That is the entire reason Quests exists: one Quest, one shared roadmap, one tap to check in, and everyone can see who has shown up today. A shared spreadsheet also works if your group is disciplined about it. What does not work is relying on memory.

Step 6: Plan for the middle

Almost every group challenge wobbles between day 10 and day 20. The novelty is gone, the finish is far, and the first person to miss two days quietly stops. Plan for it:

  • Put a shared reward at the halfway point. Dinner, a hike, a group photo, anything with a date.
  • Agree that whoever is doing best that week sends one message to whoever is struggling. A single "you okay?" restarts more streaks than any app notification.
  • Let people rejoin. A rule like "you can miss up to three days total" keeps a bad week from ending the whole thing. More on this in how to keep your streak when life gets busy.

Step 7: Finish loudly

Day 30 should be an event, even if the group is three people on a video call. Say what changed, say what was hard, and pick the next challenge while everyone is still warm. Groups that finish one challenge together tend to start a second within a week. Groups that let day 30 pass in silence usually do not.

Ideas that work well as a first group challenge

  • A step challenge, because phones already count steps and nobody has to learn anything.
  • A 30-day fitness challenge with a low daily floor.
  • Ten minutes of meditation, because it takes ten minutes.
  • A reading challenge, if your group likes talking about what they read.
  • A dry month, the classic, and much easier with company.

A note on competition

Some groups thrive on a leaderboard. Others find it stressful. Ask before you start. Quests has a friends leaderboard with points, streaks and check-in counts, and it also works fine if you never look at it. The check-ins are the point. The ranking is optional.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good challenge to do with friends?

Anything with a clear daily action and an end date: a daily walk or step target, 30 days without alcohol, ten minutes of meditation, 20 pages of reading, or a 30-day fitness challenge. Pick the one the least motivated person in the group can do on a bad day.

How do you keep a group challenge going?

Give the check-ins a dedicated home where everyone's status is visible, agree in advance that a missed day is a dent and does not end the challenge, and put a shared reward at the halfway mark. Most drop-offs happen between day 10 and day 20.

What app can you use for a challenge with friends?

Quests is built for this: you create a Quest with a daily rule and end date, invite friends by link, and everyone checks in once a day on a shared roadmap. It is free on iOS and Android.

How many friends should be in a challenge?

Two to five. That is enough for accountability and small enough that every check-in is noticed. Split larger groups into pods.
