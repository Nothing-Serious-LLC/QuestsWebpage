How Long Does It Take to Build a Habit? What the Research Says

Habit science Updated Oct 8, 2026 · By Quests

Habit formation has a variable timeline. A widely cited study estimated a median of 66 days to approach a plateau in automaticity, with individual estimates ranging from 18 to 254 days. Those figures describe a particular study and measure; they are not a deadline for your next habit.

What the 66-day finding measured

Lally and colleagues asked 96 volunteers to repeat a chosen daily behavior in a consistent context for 12 weeks. The researchers modeled how automatic the behavior felt over time. The 66-day figure refers to the median estimated time to reach 95% of the modeled plateau among participants whose data fit the curve well. Some estimates extended beyond the observation period.

The paper also reported that missing one opportunity did not materially affect the habit-formation process. It does not establish a universal rule about how many missed days are acceptable.

Read the original study and the researcher's explanation of the finding. Automaticity is a gradual quality of behavior, and it can vary across people and activities.

Choose a review date you can use

A seven-day or 30-day challenge gives a group a clear period to plan together. At the end, ask whether the activity fits your schedule, whether the cue is reliable and whether you want to continue. A completed challenge does not establish that the behavior has become automatic.

For example, a reading group could try ten minutes after dinner for a week, then discuss what happened. If dinner varies too much, each person could choose a different habit cue. The next round tests the revised plan.

Track the action and the circumstances

Keep a record of planned opportunities and what happened. A brief note about timing or location can help explain why an action fit on one day and became difficult on another. Use a weekly review to choose one adjustment.

A streak describes consecutive recorded completions. Feeling that an action happens automatically is a different observation. Both can be useful, and neither alone tells the whole story of a routine.

Plan the next opportunity after a miss

If you miss a planned action, identify the next realistic opportunity. Check whether the original plan is still appropriate. A smaller action, a different setting or a pause may fit better than trying to make up every missed session.
