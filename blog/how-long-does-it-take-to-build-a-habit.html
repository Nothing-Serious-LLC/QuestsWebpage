How Long Does It Take to Build a Habit? What the Research Says

Science Sep 5, 2026 · 5 min read

You have probably heard that it takes 21 days to form a habit. The number comes from a 1960 book by a plastic surgeon who noticed patients took about three weeks to get used to a new face. It was never a study of habits.

The 66-day finding

The most cited real study is from Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London, published in 2010. Ninety-six volunteers each chose a new daily behavior tied to a cue, such as "drink a glass of water after breakfast" or "run for 15 minutes before dinner," and reported every day for 12 weeks on how automatic it felt.

On average, behaviors reached their plateau of automaticity after 66 days. The spread was wide: 18 days for the quickest, an estimated 254 for the slowest. Simple behaviors like drinking water became automatic fast. Effortful ones like exercise took much longer.

Three things the study got right that the 21-day rule gets wrong

Habits form on a curve, and the curve flattens

Early repetitions produce big gains in automaticity. Later ones produce smaller gains. This is why the first two weeks feel like progress and weeks four to eight feel like nothing is happening. Something is happening; the curve is just flat.

Missing one day did not matter

Participants who skipped a single day showed no measurable setback. A long gap did hurt. The lesson is the one every streak app should print on its home screen: never miss twice.

Complexity sets the timeline

If you are trying to build a running habit, plan for months. If you are trying to add a glass of water, plan for weeks. Choose your challenge length accordingly.

What this means for your next challenge

  • Thirty days is a start, not a finish. A 30-day challenge gets you through the steep part of the curve. Plan a second round if the habit still feels effortful on day 30.
  • Attach the habit to a cue. Every behavior in the study was tied to a daily event. "After coffee" is a cue. "Sometime today" is a wish.
  • Track the days. Seeing 40 check-ins is what carries you across the flat part of the curve when the feeling of progress fades.
  • Do it with people. Automaticity comes from repetition, and repetition is easier with company. See why social accountability works.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 21-day habit rule true?

No. It traces back to a 1960 self-help book, not a habit study. The best available research found an average of 66 days, with a wide range depending on the behavior.

Does missing a day reset my habit?

A single missed day had no measurable effect in the UCL study. Longer gaps did. Aim to never miss two days in a row.

Why do exercise habits take longer?

The more effort a behavior takes, the more repetitions it needs to feel automatic. Simple, low-effort actions tied to a strong cue form fastest.
