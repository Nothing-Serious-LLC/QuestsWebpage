How to Run a Workplace Wellness Challenge People Actually Join

Guide Sep 16, 2026 · 7 min read

Most workplace wellness challenges are announced with enthusiasm, joined by a third of the team, and forgotten by week two. The problem is rarely the idea. It is the setup: too many people in one group, a habit that only suits the already fit, and check-ins that live in a channel nobody opens.

Here is a format that works for teams of five to fifty, in an office or fully remote.

Pick a habit that fits a workday

The habit has to be doable by someone with back-to-back meetings, a commute and kids. That rules out anything that needs an hour. Good options:

  • Step target. 7,000 a day. Phones already count it. The step challenge guide covers fair rules.
  • Walking meetings. One call a day taken on foot.
  • Movement break. 20 minutes of any exercise, any time.
  • Water. Two litres before the end of the workday.
  • Lunch away from the desk. Thirty minutes, screen off.
  • Ten minutes of meditation or breathing. Quiet, and easy to fit before the first call.
  • Screens off by 10 p.m. A sleep challenge that respects that the team is tired.

Avoid anything about weight, diet or body measurements. They exclude people, raise privacy problems, and are the reason many people distrust workplace wellness in the first place.

Make it opt-in and private

Three rules that protect the challenge and the people in it:

  1. Nobody is enrolled by default. Announce it, share the link, let people join.
  2. Check-ins are visible only inside the pod. No company-wide leaderboard with names. A team total is fine.
  3. No manager reporting. A wellness challenge that feeds into anything performance-related stops being a wellness challenge.

Say these out loud in the announcement. Participation goes up when people know what they are agreeing to.

Use pods of four

A challenge with 30 people in one group has no accountability: nobody notices a missing check-in in a list that long. Split into pods of three to five, mixed across teams. Each pod runs its own Quest with the same rule. The research on social accountability is consistent that small groups outperform both solo attempts and large ones.

If you want a company-wide element, add a team total: the percentage of pod members who checked in each day, posted once a week. It creates friendly pressure without exposing anyone.

Give the check-ins a home

Slack and Teams are where check-ins go to die. A dedicated app keeps the daily status visible at a glance. Quests is built for this: create the Quest, share one link per pod, everyone checks in with one tap, and the shared roadmap shows who has done today's action. No company account or admin setup is required, which is usually what makes an HR-led challenge take a month to launch. Each pod can be running in five minutes.

The 30-day plan

Before day 1

Announce two weeks out. Share the rule, the length, the privacy rules and the pod format. Ask people to opt in by a date. Form pods and send links three days before.

Week 1

A short kick-off message from whoever is organising. Then nothing except the daily check-ins. Resist the urge to over-communicate.

Week 2

Post the team total once. Ask each pod to share one tip in a shared channel.

Week 3

The slump week. A small shared event helps: a company-wide walking hour on a Wednesday, a group photo, a five-minute guided breathing session on a call.

Week 4 and day 30

Finish with a short all-hands moment: completion rate, one story from a pod, and a vote on the next challenge. Momentum comes from starting the next one within two weeks.

Budget

Zero is fine. If there is a budget, spend it on the day 30 event or on something the whole team gets for a high completion rate. Individual prizes tilt the challenge toward the people who were going to win anyway.

Measuring whether it worked

Two numbers: the percentage of the team who opted in, and the percentage of those who checked in on at least 24 of 30 days. Anything above 50% and 60% respectively is a strong first challenge. Ask three questions at the end: would you do another, what should change, what habit next. That is your plan for round two. Frequently asked questions

What are good workplace wellness challenge ideas?

A 7,000-step target, one walking call a day, 20 minutes of movement, two litres of water before the end of the workday, lunch away from the desk, ten minutes of meditation, or screens off by 10 p.m. Pick one habit that someone with a full calendar can do.

How long should a workplace wellness challenge last?

Thirty days. Long enough to form a habit, short enough to finish. Announce two weeks before, and plan the next challenge for two weeks after.

How do you get employees to join a wellness challenge?

Make it opt-in, keep check-ins private to small pods, promise no manager reporting, and choose a habit that does not require fitness or spare time. Participation rises when people know exactly what they are agreeing to.

Do we need special software for a team wellness challenge?

No. A free app like Quests lets each pod run its own challenge with a shared daily check-in and no company admin setup. A shared spreadsheet also works if the group is small and disciplined.
