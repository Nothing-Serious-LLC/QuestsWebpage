How to Plan an Optional Workplace Wellness Challenge

Guide Updated Nov 23, 2026 · 7 min read

A workplace wellness challenge starts with an optional activity, a realistic schedule and clear sharing boundaries. Ask colleagues what they want to try, agree on the rules and review a small first round before extending it. A two-week pilot gives the group a defined point to decide what happens next.

Choose an activity people can adapt

Offer a few choices and let participants set an appropriate amount. Availability, accessibility, caring responsibilities and personal preferences can differ across a team.

  • A planned break: choose an available break and decide how to spend it.
  • Personal movement: choose a suitable action and personal target. For a walking format, use the step-challenge planning guide.
  • Creative practice: try a short drawing, writing or observation exercise. The remote-colleague practice plan includes six optional prompts and a worksheet.
  • Reading: choose a short reading session, with an optional conversation afterward.
  • A workday transition: write tomorrow's first task before finishing work, if that fits the person's role.

Keep health outcomes outside the scoring rules. Weight, food restriction and body measurements require different expertise and raise personal concerns that this planning guide does not resolve. People should be able to take part through an action that suits them or decline altogether.

Write the invitation before choosing a tool

Here is an original invitation you can adapt:

We are trying an optional two-week practice group. Choose one small activity and the days that fit your schedule. Sharing a reflection is optional. You can change your plan or leave at any time. Participation and individual records will stay separate from work evaluation. Interested people can reply privately by Friday.

Only make privacy commitments you can honor in the chosen workplace and tool. Decide who can view messages, whether a manager can access the space, and how participants can stop sharing. Ask permission before publishing names, photographs or group results.

Pick a manageable group format

There is no universal ideal group size in this guide. Start with the people who opt in and enough facilitation capacity to answer questions. A larger group may choose smaller voluntary discussion circles. Give each circle the same written expectations and a contact for practical questions.

Keep one copy of the plan in a place participants can reach. A shared document, existing chat or challenge app can support different needs. Review tool choices and the Quests product guide before selecting a setup. Verify its actual visibility settings with the group.

A worked two-week pilot

In this fictional example, six colleagues volunteer. Four choose a drawing practice on Tuesday and Thursday; two choose reading on Monday, Wednesday and Friday. Each person sets their own session length. They arrange one optional end-of-week conversation.

  • Before the start: agree on dates, personal actions, a contact and sharing boundaries. Use the kickoff agenda to record decisions.
  • First week: try the chosen schedule. Record practical obstacles privately or share them voluntarily.
  • Midpoint: ask whether the plan fits. Record any changed schedule so the final review uses the right denominator.
  • Second week: try the revised plan. A missed session can stay visible in the record without an extra penalty.
  • Close: invite feedback and let each person choose whether to continue, change activity or stop.

Measure the plan with clear denominators

The four drawing participants each plan four sessions, giving 16 opportunities. The two reading participants each plan six sessions, giving 12. There are 28 planned opportunities in total. If the voluntarily supplied records show 12 drawing sessions and 9 reading sessions completed, that is 21 of 28, or 75%.

This invented result describes recorded actions in this example. It establishes no improvement in employee health, productivity or morale. If records are missing, keep them marked unknown and describe the coverage. Avoid comparing teams with different activities or recording methods. The completion-rate guide explains the calculation.

A short closing discussion can ask: Did the schedule fit? Was sharing comfortable? Which part should change? Keep the response optional. A participant may value the experience without wanting another round.

Budget and follow-through

Begin with materials people already have and a time commitment they explicitly choose. Check any workplace requirements with the relevant person before offering paid work time, incentives or a company-sponsored program.

For the next round, revise one practical issue from the feedback. Keep the participation agreement current, and use fair group rules for changed schedules, late arrivals and optional sharing.

Frequently asked questions

What are good workplace wellness challenge ideas?

Offer adaptable choices such as a planned break, suitable personal movement, reading, creative practice or a workday transition. Let people choose an appropriate action and schedule.

How long should a workplace wellness challenge last?

Choose a bounded first round and a review date. The worked example uses two weeks to test the plan. No fixed duration guarantees a habit or a health outcome.

How do you invite employees without pressure?

Make participation optional, explain who can see shared information, keep it separate from performance evaluation and provide a clear way to change plans or leave.

What should we measure?

Count the planned opportunities and the voluntarily recorded completed actions. State missing coverage and gather optional feedback. These measures describe participation in the plan.
